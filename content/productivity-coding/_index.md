# AI Productivity and Coding Tools: What Each One Is Actually For

This category has been reshaped by agents — software that does not just autocomplete but plans, edits, runs and iterates on your behalf. That shift has brought remarkable capability and equally remarkable growing pains: two of the four tools here made headlines in 2025 and 2026 for pricing changes that angered their own users, and one for an AI agent that deleted a production database. The capability is real, and so is the need to read the fine print, which is what these reviews try to give you.

The four tools cover distinct jobs: an AI code editor for professional developers, a browser-based app builder aimed increasingly at non-coders, a no-code website builder, and Notion's project-management side seen through the lens of its new agents. Each review carries a verification date, treats every price as second-hand until you confirm it, and is frank about the incidents and complaints as well as the strengths.

## Writing and shipping code

**Cursor** is the AI code editor of the moment, built by Anysphere on a VS Code foundation and now a heavily funded, agentic IDE with its own models. Its review covers the signature Tab autocomplete, Agent Mode and the newer parallel-agent workflow, and it deals squarely with the June 2025 pricing change that drew a backlash and a public apology — plus the extraordinary, widely reported and still-pending SpaceX acquisition, framed as reported rather than done.

**Replit** approaches coding from the opposite end: a browser-based platform pivoting hard towards "vibe coding" for people who are not developers. The review treats the July 2025 incident in which its Agent deleted a production database as the cautionary tale it is, alongside the 2026 shift to effort-based pricing that left some users with unpredictable bills, while still crediting the speed of going from prompt to deployed app.

## Building a website without code

**Framer** turned a design-and-prototyping tool into a full AI-assisted website builder, with generation features like Wireframer and Workshop now reaching onto the canvas in its 3.0 release. The review is honest about the recurring complaints — vendor lock-in with no code export, per-site and per-seat pricing that adds up for agencies, and the October 2025 tier overhaul — and it clears up the common confusion with Motion, the animation library that was spun off and renamed.

## Managing projects with agents

**Notion (Projects & Agents)** is covered here for its project-management side specifically — databases, Timeline and dependencies, sprints — and for the agents that now act on that data, from the autonomous personal Agent introduced in Notion 3.0 to the trigger-based Custom Agents metered by credits since May 2026. The review keeps the writing-assistant material to a companion Notion AI review to avoid overlap, and it is balanced about where Notion strains against dedicated tools like Jira or MS Project on large, resource-heavy programmes, plus the agent-security disclosures that come with letting software act on your workspace.

## Two things worth remembering before you commit

First, pricing in this corner of the market is not just changeable but structurally tricky: usage-based credits, effort-based billing and per-seat fees mean the sticker price rarely equals what you pay in a busy month. Every figure in these reviews is a reported starting point to confirm on the vendor's page, and it is worth setting spending caps where a tool offers them. Second, agents are powerful precisely because they act without asking at each step, which is exactly why the incidents here matter — keep backups, use planning or preview modes where they exist, and give an autonomous agent access to production systems only when you have understood what it can do on its own.
