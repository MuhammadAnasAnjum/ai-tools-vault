# Suno AI Review: The Consumer Music Generator Under a Legal Cloud

Type a few words about the song you want, and Suno hands you back a finished track — vocals, melody, arrangement, and all. For a lot of people that first generation is a genuinely startling moment: the gap between "I have an idea" and "I have a song" collapses to seconds. It is the reason Suno has become the most talked-about consumer AI music tool, with a subscriber base and revenue that dwarf most rivals.

This review covers what Suno does, how good it has become with its latest model, and the subject no honest write-up can skip: the copyright litigation that defines its present. That legal picture is nuanced and still unresolved, so this piece states precisely what is settled, what is ongoing, and what remains genuinely undecided rather than guessing at an outcome.

## What is Suno?

Suno is an AI music generator built by the company Suno. It creates complete songs, including sung vocals, from a text prompt describing the style, mood, and subject you have in mind. Its current model, v5.5, was released on 26 March 2026, and the tool has grown into a major consumer product — reportedly around 2 million subscribers and roughly $300M in annual recurring revenue, figures that put it well ahead of most competitors in reach.

The experience is deliberately simple. You describe a song, choose a rough genre or vibe, and Suno produces one or more full tracks you can then extend, regenerate, or tweak. That accessibility is the whole point: it lowers music creation to something anyone can attempt, whether for fun, for social clips, or as a starting sketch for more serious work.

The complication is legal, and it is central rather than incidental. Suno sits at the heart of a high-stakes copyright dispute with the major record labels, and anyone considering building on the platform should understand where that stands before relying on it commercially. The next sections lay out both the product and that context.

## Key Features

- **Text-to-song generation** — describe a track in plain language and receive a full song with vocals, melody, and arrangement.
- **Model v5.5** — the current generation, released 26 March 2026, bringing improved audio quality and coherence over earlier versions.
- **Vocal generation** — produces sung lyrics rather than instrumental beds alone, the capability that sets it apart from many tools.
- **Song extension** — takes a generated or uploaded snippet and continues it, building a short idea into a longer piece.
- **Style and genre control** — steer the output towards particular genres, moods, and instrumentation through the prompt.
- **Custom lyrics** — supply your own words or let Suno write them, then hear them performed in the chosen style.
- **Regeneration and variations** — spin up alternative takes on the same prompt to find the version that fits best.
- **Web and app access** — create from the browser or mobile, keeping generations in your account library.

## Pros and Cons

**What works well**

On pure capability, Suno is the most impressive consumer music tool available. The v5.5 model produces songs that are coherent, catchy, and — for casual listening — surprisingly convincing, and the ability to generate real vocals rather than instrumentals alone is what puts it ahead of the field. The barrier to entry is almost nonexistent: someone with no musical training can describe a mood and have a finished track in moments. For rapid ideation, background music for videos, and simply exploring what a song idea might sound like, it is genuinely remarkable, and its scale means it is well-funded and actively developed.

**Where it falls short**

The overriding reservation is legal uncertainty, and it is serious enough to shape how you should use the tool. Beyond that, the creative limits of prompt-driven generation still show: fine control over specific arrangements is limited, outputs can feel formulaic on close listening, and it is a tool for generating songs rather than a substitute for a producer's craft. For anyone intending to release or monetise music, the unresolved litigation adds a layer of risk that a purely creative review cannot wave away.

**The copyright situation — read this carefully**

This is the defining issue, so precision matters. The RIAA lawsuit against Suno is now split. Warner Music settled and signed a licensing deal with Suno on 25 November 2025. However, Sony Music and Universal Music are still litigating, in the US District Court for the District of Massachusetts before Judge Casper. In May 2026 the plaintiffs expanded the case to roughly 61,026 recordings, with potential statutory damages reported to exceed $9 billion. A fair-use hearing was scheduled for July 2026, but as of 9 August 2026 there is no confirmed ruling — the matter is pending and the outcome remains genuinely open. For contrast, the rival tool Udio settled with both Sony and Universal, leaving Suno as the one still contesting the point.

## How to Use Suno

1. **Sign in** — create an account at suno.com and open the creation screen from the browser or the mobile app.
2. **Describe your song** — write a prompt covering the genre, mood, tempo, and subject you want the track to capture.
3. **Choose the model** — generate on v5.5, the current release, for the best audio quality and coherence.
4. **Add lyrics** — supply your own words or let Suno write them, keeping them in step with the style you asked for.
5. **Generate and compare** — produce a couple of variations, listen through, and pick the take that best fits your idea.
6. **Extend or refine** — lengthen a promising snippet with the extension tool, or adjust the prompt and regenerate for a different feel.
7. **Check the licensing terms** — before any public or commercial use, read Suno's current terms on ownership and rights given the ongoing litigation.
8. **Export your track** — download the finished song from your library, keeping a note of the terms that applied when you made it.

### FAQs

**Is Suno free?**
There is a free tier that lets you generate songs and get a feel for the tool, with paid plans lifting the limits. Reported pricing puts Pro around ~$10 a month and Premier around ~$30, though older reviews that call the ~$30 tier "Pro" are out of date. Confirm the current tiers and what each allows on Suno's pricing page.

**Can I legally sell or release music made with Suno?**
This is the hard question, and the honest answer is that it carries real uncertainty. Because Sony and Universal are still litigating over the training data behind these models, the legal footing for commercial release is unsettled. Read Suno's own terms carefully and, for anything with money attached, consider taking professional advice before you publish.

**What exactly is the lawsuit status right now?**
It is split. Warner settled and signed a licensing deal with Suno on 25 November 2025, but Sony and Universal are still litigating in the District of Massachusetts before Judge Casper. The case was expanded in May 2026 to around 61,026 recordings, and a fair-use hearing set for July 2026 has, as of 9 August 2026, produced no confirmed ruling — it remains pending.

**How does Suno compare with Udio?**
Both generate songs from prompts, but they diverge sharply on the legal front: Udio settled with both Sony and Universal, while Suno continues to contest the labels' claims. On raw capability Suno is widely regarded as the most powerful consumer option, but that unresolved litigation is the trade-off to weigh.
