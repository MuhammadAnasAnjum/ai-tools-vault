# AI Writing and Research Tools: What Each One Is Actually For

This category is the messiest in the directory, because almost every tool in it has changed what it does since the reviews you will find elsewhere were written. Two rebranded, two pivoted to entirely different products, and one had its development quietly halted while remaining on sale.

Sorting them by what they are for today, rather than by what they were built for, is the only way this category makes sense.

## Fixing writing you have already done

**Grammarly** remains the best mainstream checker, and it is the tool most often described inaccurately right now — its parent company renamed itself Superhuman in October 2025, though the writing product is still Grammarly. Its generative features absorbed the old GrammarlyGO branding. Read the review for the billing structure specifically: the advertised per-month price is the annual rate, and refunds are issued only where legally required.

**Wordtune** does sentence-level rewriting better than almost anything else, and comes with a warning attached. AI21 Labs halted its development in April 2025 while continuing to sell it, and the company has since cut more than 60% of its staff. It works today. It is not being improved, and annual billing on it is a poor idea.

## Writing at volume for a team

**Jasper** survived the ChatGPT shock by becoming something else: a marketing platform built on brand voice, shared knowledge and repeatable campaign workflows rather than a copy generator. Unlimited words are now standard and Boss Mode is retired. It only makes sense for teams needing consistency at volume — a solo writer will find a general assistant cheaper and comparable.

**Copy.ai** went further and is no longer a copywriting tool at all. It is a go-to-market automation platform, and its pricing has a gap rather than a ladder: an entry chat plan around $29 a month that excludes the workflow product, then a jump into four figures. If you arrived looking for the tool that wrote social captions, it is gone.

## Getting found inside AI answers

**Writesonic** made the most forward-looking pivot in this group, repositioning around generative engine optimisation — tracking whether AI assistants mention your brand when someone asks a relevant question. The legacy article writer still ships, and Chatsonic and Botsonic remain active as separate products. Its pricing is the least reliably documented of anything here.

## Writing in your own voice

**HyperWrite** solves a problem the large assistants have not: it learns your writing from samples and holds that voice persistently, so output does not need rewriting to sound like you. It also has browser automation that predated most competitors. The review covers one credibility question about its parent company's public performance claims, which is relevant when weighing its marketing.

## Academic work

**Jenni AI** is built for essays, literature reviews and theses, and its best design decision is drawing citations from a real research catalogue rather than model memory. That reduces fabricated references without eliminating them — verify every one. The review also sets out the academic integrity position properly, including why the detection statistics circulating about it are worthless: they come from companies selling detector-bypass tools.

## Research and fact-finding

**Perplexity Pro** has a dedicated decision page here rather than a second product review, since our main Perplexity write-up lives in the chatbots category. It answers one question — whether the $20 monthly subscription is worth it — and covers what free now includes, why the per-day quotas quoted elsewhere are obsolete, and which free-access promotions have already expired.

## Budget and short-form copy

**Rytr** is the cheapest credible AI writer here, tuned for short-form copy — emails, ad lines, product descriptions — rather than long articles. Its review also covers the one thing that made news: the FTC filed a complaint in 2024 over its review-generation feature, and the resulting order was reopened and set aside in December 2025. We cover that neutrally: it concerned one narrow feature, involved no admission of liability, and the reversal was driven by a shift in enforcement policy. Using any tool to fabricate reviews remains deceptive regardless.

## All-in-one platforms

**HIX.AI** bundles a long-form writer, chat assistant, browser extension and template library under one subscription, metered by credits. Its review deals directly with HIX Bypass, the separately branded "AI humanizer" the company markets — a legitimate editing aid for content you own, but misconduct if used to disguise AI authorship where that is prohibited. It also sets the record straight on why AI detectors are unreliable, which is an argument against trusting them, not an endorsement of paying to defeat them.

**Monica** is a browser-overlay assistant with access to several frontier models for one price — strong for everyday summarising, translating and drafting on the page you are already reading. Two caveats run through the review: its "unlimited" plans actually cap premium-model access, and its parent company, Butterfly Effect, was the subject of a Meta acquisition that Chinese regulators blocked and ordered unwound in April 2026. Ownership is unchanged; we state it as context, not a buy signal.

## Writing inside your workspace

**Notion AI** is not a standalone writer but a layer inside Notion, and its value comes from acting on the notes, docs and databases you already keep there — workspace Q&A is the headline feature. Its pricing changed most confusingly of anything here: the flat $10-per-member add-on was discontinued around May 2025, and AI is now bundled into Business and Enterprise plans with credit-based metering on top. Older reviews quoting the $10 figure describe a product that no longer sells that way.

## Creative writing and wordplay

**Google TextFX** is the odd one out — not a purchasable product but a free Lupe Fiasco x Google experiment offering roughly ten wordplay tools built for inspiration rather than output. Its status is genuinely uncertain: it ran on PaLM 2, which Google shut down in April 2025, and its code repository has been described as archived. The review deliberately does not claim it is either working or dead, only that its engine was retired — the sole reliable check is to open the site and try it.

## Three patterns worth carrying into any purchase

**Older reviews in this category are more misleading than in most.** Copy.ai, Writesonic and Jasper all sell fundamentally different products than they did three years ago, and Grammarly's company name changed. A 2023 comparison table is not slightly stale here; it describes products that no longer exist.

**The billing terms are where the complaints are, not the output.** Across Grammarly, Wordtune, Jenni and Writesonic, the recurring theme in negative reviews is refunds and renewals rather than quality: short refund windows of five to seven days, non-refundable policies, renewals charged without notice, and advertised monthly prices that are actually annual rates. Choose monthly billing while evaluating anything in this category, and diarise any refund deadline.

**Free tiers are getting quieter, not more generous.** Several vendors here have stopped publishing exact limits, moved to credit metering, or withdrawn free plans entirely. That makes it harder to compare on paper and more important to test with your own real work before committing.

Every review in this category carries a verification date, and several carry an explicit note where sources conflicted and we chose not to publish a number we could not confirm. Checking the vendor's own pricing page before subscribing is still worth the thirty seconds.
