# Google TextFX: The Creative Writing Experiment, and Its Uncertain Status

Google TextFX is not a product in the way the other tools in this category are. It was a free creative-writing experiment — a set of playful language tools built with the rapper Lupe Fiasco to help writers, lyricists and wordsmiths explore language rather than generate finished copy.

It is worth documenting for two reasons. It is a genuinely interesting example of what AI-assisted creativity can look like when the goal is inspiration rather than output. And its status is now in real doubt, because the model it was built on has been shut down. This piece explains what it was, what it did, and how to think about whether it still works — without pretending to certainty the sources do not support.

## What TextFX was

TextFX launched in 2023 as part of Google Lab Sessions, a series pairing outside creators with Google's AI teams. Built with Lupe Fiasco, it packaged around ten small tools, each aimed at a specific creative-language task rather than at drafting whole pieces. The framing was explicitly experimental: a sandbox for exploring how a language model could support the creative process, offered free and without the trappings of a commercial product.

Crucially, it ran on PaLM 2, Google's language model of that era. That technical detail, unremarkable at launch, is now the whole question mark over the tool.

## The ten tools, and what they did

Each TextFX tool took a word or phrase and returned creative variations to spark ideas:

- **Simile** — generated comparisons for a given thing.
- **Explode** — broke a word into similar-sounding words and phrases.
- **Unexpect** — made a scene more surprising or surreal.
- **Chain** — built a chain of associated words.
- **POV** — offered opinions on a topic from invented perspectives.
- **Alliteration** — produced alliterative phrases around a topic.
- **Acronym** — turned a word into an acronym.
- **Fuse** — found conceptual overlap between two things.
- **Scene** — built sensory detail around a setting.
- **Unfold** — connected a word to a larger related concept.

The point was never a polished paragraph. It was to hand a writer unexpected raw material — a strange simile, an unlikely association — to break a block or find an angle. Used that way, it was a small, generous, genuinely creative thing.

## Why its status is uncertain

TextFX was built on PaLM 2. Google deprecated the PaLM family, and access to those models ended on 9 April 2025, with developers directed to migrate to newer models. Any service still relying on PaLM 2 after that date would have needed re-plumbing onto a current model to keep working.

Public sources do not confirm whether TextFX was migrated, quietly retired, or left up in a degraded state. The experiment's associated code repository has been described as deprecated or archived. So the honest position is: the backend it originally used no longer exists, and there is no reliable confirmation that the hosted experience still functions as it did. The page may still load; the tools behind it may or may not respond as originally designed.

If you want to know its state today, the only reliable check is to open textfx.withgoogle.com yourself and try a tool. This review will not claim it works or that it is gone, because the evidence supports neither with confidence — only that its original engine was shut down and its status is unverified.

## How to think about using it

1. **Treat it as inspiration, never output.** Even at its best it produced raw creative material to react to, not finished writing.
2. **Check whether it still responds** by opening the site and running one tool, rather than trusting any source — including this one — on its current state.
3. **Have a fallback in mind.** Any current general assistant can imitate these prompts — ask for ten unexpected similes, or an alliterative riff on a theme — if TextFX is unavailable or degraded.
4. **Do not build a workflow on it.** It was an experiment from the start, and experiments are withdrawn without notice; the PaLM shutdown is exactly that risk realised.
5. **Enjoy it for what it is** if it still runs — a rare example of an AI tool designed for play and craft rather than productivity.

### FAQs

**Is Google TextFX still working in 2026?**
It cannot be confirmed either way from public sources. It originally ran on PaLM 2, which Google shut down on 9 April 2025, and its code repository has been described as archived. The page may still load, but whether the tools function as designed is unverified — the only reliable check is to open the site and try one.

**What is Google TextFX?**
It was a free creative-writing experiment from Google Lab Sessions, built with Lupe Fiasco, offering around ten small tools that generate similes, alliteration, unexpected associations and other creative-language ideas. It was designed for inspiration and wordplay, not for producing finished text.

**How much does TextFX cost?**
Nothing. It was released free as an experiment, with no paid tier or subscription, which is part of why its long-term availability was never guaranteed.

**What can I use instead of TextFX?**
Any current general-purpose AI assistant can reproduce its prompts — ask for creative similes, alliterative phrases, surprising scene details or word associations. You lose the purpose-built interface but keep the creative function, and you avoid depending on an experiment that may already be retired.
